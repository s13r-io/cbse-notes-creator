# Source 3: Gemini

## Core Framing
Creating effective study notes is less about transcription and more about **encoding**—the process of getting information into your brain by actively manipulating it. When managing 8–9 subjects, your goal is to move from "passive recording" to "active processing."

---

## 1. The Process: From First Reading to Final Notes

Research suggests that "simultaneous note-taking" (writing while reading) often leads to shallow processing. Use the **SQ3R** or **PQRST** methods to structure your workflow:

- **Scan/Preview:** Spend 5 minutes looking at headings, bold terms, and diagrams to create a mental "map."
- **Read & Retrieve:** Read a section, close the book, and ask, "What did I just learn?" This is **active recall**.
- **Drafting:** Only write your notes *after* you've processed the section. This ensures you are summarizing concepts in your own words rather than copying.

---

## 2. Subject-Specific Formatting

While the core principle of active processing is universal, the **structure** should adapt to the cognitive demands of the subject:

| Subject Type | Recommended Format | Focus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Hierarchical (History/Law)** | **Outline Method** | Focuses on cause-and-effect and chronological flow. |
| **Conceptual (Biology/Psych)** | **Concept Maps** | Focuses on how different systems or terms interconnect. |
| **Problem-Based (Math/Physics)** | **Two-Column Method** | Left side for the step-by-step solution; right side for the *logic/rule* behind the step. |

---

## 3. Common Mistakes & How to Avoid Them

- **The Transcription Trap:** Copying the textbook word-for-word. **Fix:** Use the "Generation Effect"—rephrase everything into your own unique sentence structures.
- **The "Fluency" Illusion:** Thinking that because notes look clean and readable, you know the material. **Fix:** Ensure your notes contain gaps or questions that force you to think.
- **Over-Highlighting:** Highlighting is a low-utility strategy because it doesn't require mental effort. **Fix:** Trade the highlighter for a pen and write a "why this matters" note in the margin instead.

---

## 4. Balancing Depth with Efficiency

With 9 subjects, you cannot afford "total coverage." Use the **Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule)**:

- **Prioritize High-Yield Info:** Focus 80% of your note-taking effort on the 20% of content that is most complex or most likely to be examined (check past papers).
- **The "Skeleton" Technique:** If you are short on time, create a skeleton of the chapter (headings and key terms) and fill in the details only for the parts you cannot explain out loud.

---

## 5. What to Include (Beyond Facts)

Evidence shows that **multisensory encoding** improves retention. Your notes should include:

- **Cues/Questions:** Based on the **Cornell Method**, leave a wide left margin for questions. Instead of writing "Photosynthesis," write "How do plants convert light to energy?"
- **Dual Coding:** Combine words with simple, hand-drawn diagrams. The brain processes visual and verbal information through different channels, doubling your chances of recall.
- **Non-Examples:** Record what a concept *is not* to prevent common exam "distractors."
- **Metacognition:** Write down "Why did I find this part confusing?" This helps you target your next study session.

---

## 6. Post-Note Usage: Maximum Retention

Notes are not a "storage bin"; they are a "launchpad" for **Spaced Repetition**.

- **The 24-Hour Review:** Review your notes within 24 hours to interrupt the "Forgetting Curve."
- **The Blurting Method:** Take a blank sheet of paper and "blurt" out everything you remember from your notes. Then, use your notes to see what you missed (this is your "gap analysis").
- **Self-Testing:** Turn those margin questions you wrote earlier into a mini-quiz. Testing yourself is significantly more effective than re-reading.

---

## Pro-Tip

> If you're feeling overwhelmed by the volume of 8-9 subjects, remember that **brief, messy notes created through intense mental effort** are always superior to **beautiful, comprehensive notes created through passive reading.** Focus on the "mental sweat."
