# Source 5: Perplexity

## 1. A science-backed note-taking process (from first reading to final notes)

**Key principles from research:**
- Note-taking only helps when it **summarizes** in your own words and **sustains attention**, not when it just copies text verbatim.
- Re-reading without *active transformation* (e.g., turning ideas into questions or diagrams) has weak effects on retention.

Use this **read–massage–test–sharpen** cycle:

1. **Preview & prime questions**
   - Before reading, skim headings, subheadings, and summaries. Ask:
     - "What are the 3–5 key questions this chapter should answer?"
   - Research shows self-questioning (e.g., "Why does X cause Y?") improves comprehension and recall.

2. **First read: annotate lightly**
   - Read in chunks (1–2 sections at a time).
   - Mark:
     - Key definitions
     - Cause-effect relationships
     - Lists or formulas
   - Don't write full notes yet; just highlight and marginalia. This keeps your working memory available for understanding instead of copying.

3. **Second read: make "massage" notes**
   - Go section by section and **rewrite in your own words**, *without looking* at the text. This forces retrieval and generative processing.
   - Structure each section as:
     - One main idea (topic sentence)
     - 2–4 supporting points
     - **Your own example** or analogy (research shows analogies and self-generated examples boost transfer).

4. **Turn content into questions**
   - For each main idea, write:
     - Factual questions ("What is X?")
     - Conceptual questions ("How does X relate to Y?")
     - Application questions ("What would happen if…?")
   - This mirrors the **self-testing** effect: active retrieval (questions) is far stronger for long-term retention than re-reading.

5. **Final "exam-ready" notes**
   - Condense:
     - One concise page per chapter (or 2 pages if math/derivations).
     - Use bullet points, diagrams, and mnemonics.
   - Keep a **summary box** at the top/bottom with 5–7 key points you absolutely must remember. This leverages "summary notes" as a cognitive hook.

## 2. Should format change by subject?

Yes—but the **core principles** stay the same. Use a **flexible template** instead of a rigid one.

**Universal core principles (all subjects):**
- Use **active summarization** in your own words.
- Include **questions** and **self-tests**.
- Use **hierarchical structure** (headings → subheadings → bullet points).

**Subject-specific tweaks:**

- **Math / Physics**
  - Structure per *problem type*:
    - Concept
    - Formula
    - Step-by-step worked example
    - Common mistake (e.g., sign error, wrong unit)
  - Use **Cornell-style sidebars** for "when to use this method" and "what to watch for."

- **History / Social Sciences**
  - Organize by **themes or debates** (e.g., "Causes of WW1," "Interpretations of X event").
  - In each theme, note:
    - Chronology (timeline)
    - Key actors and motives
    - Contrast between different historians' views

- **Biology / Sciences**
  - Mix:
    - **Concept maps** for processes (e.g., photosynthesis, cell division).
    - **Diagrams + labels** done by hand for better visuospatial memory.
    - Short text boxes that explain *why* each step happens.

- **Languages / Theory-heavy subjects**
  - Use **tables** to compare:
    - Terms vs definitions
    - Characters vs motivations
    - Theories vs evidence
  - Research shows charting for comparison boosts retention of facts and distinctions.

## 3. Most common mistakes and how to avoid them

| Mistake | Why it's bad | How to fix |
|--------|--------------|------------|
| **Copy-pasting or copying line-by-line** | Passive transcription; no summarization or own-word processing. | Force yourself to **close the book** and rewrite in your own words. |
| **Over-decorating (coloring, underlining everything)** | Distracts attention from meaning; often just "pretty, not deep." | Use minimal, *consistent* color cues (e.g., red = definitions, blue = examples). |
| **Making notes that are too long** | Hard to review; you never actually revisit them. | Aim for **1 page per chapter** for review; keep a "short-version" during revision. |
| **Only taking notes once** | Notes that aren't reviewed repeatedly lose their benefit. | Re-visit notes using **spaced retrieval** (see section 6). |
| **Ignoring questions and self-testing** | You think you know it, but can't retrieve it under exam pressure. | At the end of each chapter, write and answer 10–15 questions. |

## 4. Balancing depth with efficiency (8–9 subjects)

Cognitive science favors **depth in a few key ideas per topic** plus **spaced, active review** over trying to capture everything at once.

Concrete strategies:

- **Tiered note depth**
  - Pass 1 (light): 1–2 pages per chapter, bullet-only, main ideas only.
  - Pass 2 (before unit test): Add 1 working example, 1 diagram, 5 questions.
  - Pass 3 (before final): Condense into 1/2 page "exam-ready" sheet.

- **Use the 80/20 rule per subject**
  - Identify the 20% of material that appears in 80% of past papers (formulas, key definitions, recurring themes).
  - Make **extra-deep notes only for those 20% chunks**; keep the rest light.

- **Shared structures across subjects**
  - Use the same overall template (heading → main idea → supporting points → questions) but vary the *content type* (diagrams for biology, worked examples for math). This reduces cognitive load from learning new formats.

## 5. What else should your notes include (beyond facts)?

Research-based "must-haves":

- **Questions**
  - Arm yourself with **self-tests**: "Explain X to a 12-year-old," "What happens if Y changes?".

- **Examples and analogies**
  - Include:
    - One **real-world** example
    - One **made-up but simple** example
  - Self-generated examples beat rote copying for understanding.

- **Diagrams and concept maps**
  - For biology, physics, chemistry: sketch cycles, circuits, structures yourself.
  - Hand-drawing diagrams improves visuospatial memory and understanding.

- **Common mistakes and "trap" notes**
  - Reserve a small box per topic:
    - "Common error: forgetting units in calcs."
    - "People confuse X with Y; remind yourself: X is..., Y is...".
  - This helps you avoid *repeated* mistakes under exam stress.

- **Connections to other chapters**
  - At the end of each chapter, add 1–2 lines:
    - "This links to chapter 5 because…"
  - Conceptual linking is a core process behind long-term retention.

## 6. How to use your notes *after* creation (for maximum retention)

Several evidence-based techniques cluster around **active recall** and **spacing**:

- **Self-testing from notes**
  - Cover the right side (or the explanation) and try to:
    - Recall definitions from their cues
    - Solve problems without peeking
  - Active recall outperforms re-reading every time.

- **Spaced practice (distributed review)**
  - After first studying a chapter: review notes at:
    - 1 day
    - 3 days
    - 7 days
    - 14 days
  - Spaced retrieval dramatically improves long-term retention.

- **Interleaving cross-subject notes**
  - When you review, don't do one subject for 3 hours straight.
  - Mix related topics from different subjects (e.g., math formulas + related physics problems).
  - Interleaving improves discrimination and problem-solving.

- **End-of-week "summary notes"**
  - Every Sunday, create a 1-page "week-end wrap-up" per high-priority subject:
    - 5 key ideas
    - 3 hardest questions you couldn't answer
  - Summarizing at a delay strengthens consolidation.

- **Exam-time "flash" version**
  - 1–2 weeks before exams, convert each chapter into:
    - A ½-page sheet
    - A small set of flash cards (terms ↔ definitions, questions ↔ answers)
  - Compact, high-signal notes reduce cognitive load right before the test.

## Putting it all together for your context

Given that you handle **8–9 subjects**, lean into:

- A **universal template** for structure (topic → main idea → bullets → questions → errors).
- **Very light first notes** (1–2 pages per chapter), then **single-pass deepening** before unit tests.
- **Active self-testing** and **spaced reviews** instead of "more pages." This keeps cognitive load under control while maximizing retention across many subjects.
